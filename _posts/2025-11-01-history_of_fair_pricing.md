While discussing fairness in pricing may appear ironic — since prices are often viewed as purely market-driven — the history of economic thought reveals that fairness has always been central to how societies legitimize prices, from Aristotle’s just exchange and Aquinas’s just price to modern algorithmic pricing.

Aristotle and Aquinas
======
[Justum Pretium](http://pm.nlx.com.ezproxy.cul.columbia.edu/xtf/view?docId=aquinas/aquinas.02.xml;chunk.id=id405292;toc.depth=1;toc.id=id1375176;brand=default)

Pricing often feels like the least moral part of economic life. It seems to belong to markets, not to ethics. Yet the history of economic thought tells a very different story. Long before data-driven auctions, dynamic pricing, or algorithmic fairness, philosophers and theologians were already asking the same question: *What makes a price fair?*  

This question begins with **Aristotle’s notion of just exchange** in the *Nicomachean Ethics*. For Aristotle, exchange was not a neutral transaction but a moral relationship governed by proportional equality. Justice in exchange required that both parties stand in balance—each giving and receiving in proportion to the other’s contribution. When one side gained disproportionately, the moral symmetry of the relationship was broken. Money, in Aristotle’s account, was invented to make this proportionality possible: it became a common measure through which otherwise incommensurable goods could be compared. Exchange, therefore, was a matter of *commutative justice*, the correction of inequality through equivalence.  

Centuries later, **Thomas Aquinas** translated this philosophical idea into the moral economy of medieval Christian Europe. In the *Summa Theologica* (II–II, Q.77), he asked what this “just exchange” would mean in the concrete world of merchants, artisans, and prices. His answer was the concept of the *Justum Pretium*—the *just price*. To sell something for more than it is worth, or to buy it for less, Aquinas argued, is “in itself unjust and unlawful, because it introduces inequality into an exchange.” The just price was not whatever the market would bear; it was the value that preserved equality between buyer and seller and upheld the common good of the community.  

At the same time, Aquinas recognized that no society could determine this price with absolute precision. “The just price of things,” he wrote, “is not fixed with mathematical precision, but depends on a kind of estimate, so that a slight addition or subtraction would not seem to destroy the equality of justice.” A fair price, in other words, was not a single number but a moral range—an interval within which exchange remained balanced, honest, and socially acceptable. What mattered was not mathematical accuracy but ethical proportionality.  

In this way, the **just price** gave numerical form to what Aristotle had described as **just exchange**. The former was the quantitative expression of the latter’s relational ideal. Where Aristotle theorized the justice of relationships, Aquinas turned that justice into a principle of value. What both shared was the conviction that markets, left entirely to competition or desire, could not sustain fairness by themselves. Fairness had to be built into the very logic of exchange.  

Seen from this perspective, today’s debates about algorithmic fairness in pricing are not new at all. They are a return to an old question: how can the prices we compute—whether through human judgment or machine learning—remain faithful to the moral equality that makes exchange possible in the first place?  

*“To sell a thing for more than its worth, or to buy it for less than its worth, is in itself unjust and unlawful, because it introduces inequality into an exchange.”*  
— *Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica*, II–II, Q.77, Art.1
