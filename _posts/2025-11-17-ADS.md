# Understanding Interventional Evaluation of Automated Decision Systems

Algorithmic Decision Systems (ADS) are computational tools that support or guide decisions using predictions such as risk scores or recommendations. They increasingly influence high-stakes decisions across domains such as criminal justice, healthcare, child welfare, and lending. For instance, you can think of COMPAS, a risk assessment tool used in some U.S. courts to estimate a defendant’s likelihood of reoffending and to inform pretrial release decisions. Importantly, COMPAS does not determine pretrial outcomes. It simply provides information that human judges may consider when formulating their decisions.

Once these systems shape decisions and outcomes, evaluating their real-world impact becomes far more complex than evaluating predictive accuracy alone.

## Why Interventional Evaluation Is Needed

Traditional evaluations of predictive models focus on accuracy, calibration, or error rates. But ADS often inform or shape human decisions, and those decisions then influence downstream outcomes. Because of this, standard prediction metrics do not translate directly to interventional (decision-making) settings. In other words, a model with high predictive accuracy does not necessarily lead to good or optimal decisions.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) offer the strongest form of evidence for understanding how ADS affect real outcomes, but they can be resource-intensive, ethically challenging, or simply infeasible in many institutional environments.

The above paradigm highlights three broad interventional evaluation approaches:

### **(1) ITR Evaluation (blue highlight)**  
Evaluate how changes in **decisions (D)** affect **outcomes (Y)**.  
This is the core "individualized treatment rule" (ITR) perspective.

### **(2) Event-Study / Quasi-Experimental Designs (green highlight)**  
Treat the introduction of an ADS as a **policy intervention** and compare outcomes **before vs. after** deployment.

### **(3) Micro-Level Analysis (red highlight)**  
When micro-data is available, analyze interactions between:
- risk scores **R**,  
- decisions **D**, and  
- outcomes **Y**.

Each approach addresses different parts of the causal pathway depending on available data and organizational constraints.


## (1) ITR Evaluation

### Why Evaluating ITR Performance Is Off-Policy Evaluation

Evaluating an ITR asks:
> *“What would outcomes look like if we used this new rule instead of historical decisions?”*

But the historical data was generated under previous human decision-making, not the new rule.
Thus:
- We only observe outcomes under the old decisions.
- We do not observe the counterfactual outcomes under the proposed rule.

This is why ITR evaluation is framed as off-policy evaluation — the same challenge seen in reinforcement learning and policy optimization.

### Translating Prediction Metrics into Intervention Metrics

Metrics like **true positive rate (TPR)** must be reinterpreted when decisions act as interventions.

In prediction:
- TPR = P(Ŷ=1 | Y=1).

In interventional settings, the analogous concept becomes:
- Would an individual’s outcome **improve** under the decision?

This uses potential outcomes:
- \(Y(1)\): outcome if they receive the intervention  
- \(Y(0)\): outcome if they do not

A fairness metric shown in the screenshot compares treatment of individuals who would benefit:
\[
P(D = 1 \mid Y(1) > Y(0), A = 1) 
-
P(D = 1 \mid Y(1) > Y(0), A = 0)
\]

This is called a **Responder TPR Disparity**.

### The Fundamental Identifiability Challenge
We **never** observe both \(Y(0)\) and \(Y(1)\) for the same person.  
Therefore the event \(\{Y(1) > Y(0)\}\) — those who benefit — is unobserved.

## (2) Quasi-Experimental and Observational Evaluation of ADS

### Event-Studies (Pre-Post Comparisons)
Analyze outcomes before and after ADS deployment.  
Example from the screenshots:

- Kentucky’s HB463 instructed judges to consider risk levels.
- Before-after and difference-in-differences analyses reveal:
  - minimal racial differences pre-HB463,
  - but post-HB463, **white defendants received more lenient bond decisions** than Black defendants in comparable risk categories.

### Micro-Level Data Approaches

When \((R, D, Y)\) are available at the individual level:

- Analysts can model how risk scores shape decisions.
- They can study mechanisms: does the ADS actually influence decision-makers?

## 6. Experimental Evaluation: Randomized Provision of ADS

Some deployments randomize whether a decision-maker **sees** the risk score.  
This allows direct causal inference.

### Case Study: Dane County, Wisconsin (2017–2019)

Judges were randomly assigned to see or not see the Public Safety Assessment (PSA).  
Findings from the screenshots:

- PSA visibility led to **more lenient decisions for female defendants**.  
- Male defendants’ outcomes were largely unchanged.  
- No strong evidence of disparate racial impact.

This illustrates how randomized provision can isolate the effect of ADS on decisions and outcomes.

---

### Human Decision-Makers and Unobserved Confounders

Institutional decision-making involves human judgment that may rely on information not captured in administrative data.  
These **unobserved confounders** can bias causal estimates.

The screenshots emphasize:

- Analysts must reason with domain knowledge or use tools like sensitivity analysis.
- Some settings require instrumental variables or proxy strategies.
- Understanding how humans rely on risk scores is itself an important research question.

---

### When Models Deterministically Set Decisions

If decisions follow a clear rule based on predictions, e.g.:

\[
D = g(\hat{R})
\]

then evaluation can use **Regression Discontinuity Designs (RDDs)** around decision thresholds.  
However, high-dimensional ML models introduce challenges for classical RDD assumptions.

## Conclusions

Across all sections, a central theme emerges:

> Evaluating ADS requires both understanding how decisions change and how those decisions affect outcomes.

Depending on the data and context, analysts may combine:

- Off-policy ITR evaluation  
- Event studies  
- Quasi-experimental methods  
- Experimental trials  
- Micro-level behavioral analysis

No single method fits all situations, but together they form a toolkit for rigorous assessment of ADS in practice.
